Population is much more straightforward than FDI, exchange rates, or external-sector flows, but there are still a few traps. The biggest one is accidentally treating population like a flow.

For the World Bank SP.POP.TOTL series, total population is an annual mid-year estimate based on the de facto population concept.

So I would keep the Population family to 3 canonical bases, with one optional annualized representation inside Basis C.

Population family
Metric

Population, total

$$ P_{i,t} $$

where \(P\) is the country's estimated population at the reference point of year \(t\).

Because the World Bank series is a mid-year population estimate, the values are stocks at a point in time, not annual flows.

That determines the methodology.

Basis A — Annual Population
Formula

Simply use the World Bank value:

$$ AnnualPopulation_{i,t}=P_{i,t} $$
Example

Suppose in 2025:

Country	Population
India	1.46B
China	1.41B
USA	347M

These are valid cross-country comparisons of population size.

Ranking

Largest population → rank 1.

So:

$$ Rank_i=rank_{\downarrow}(P_{i,t}) $$
But the UI must be explicit

This is a ranking of:

Population size

not:

population growth, development, demographic performance, economic importance, or living standards.

A larger population is not inherently "better."

Benchmark

You can provide the leave-one-out peer average:

$$ Benchmark_i= \frac{\sum_{j\ne i}P_j}{n-1} $$

and:

$$ Gap_i=P_i-Benchmark_i $$

Unit:

people

Example:

India = 1.46B
Peer average = 180M

$$ Gap=+1.28B $$

Meaning:

India's population is 1.28 billion above the eligible-economy average.

That is descriptive, not a performance judgment.

Basis B — Period Population Change

Population is a stock, so the correct period change is an endpoint difference.

For Start \(S\) and End \(E\):

$$ \Delta P_i=P_{i,E}-P_{i,S} $$
Example

Suppose:

$$ P_{2004}=1.10B $$ $$ P_{2014}=1.30B $$

Then:

$$ \Delta P=1.30-1.10=0.20B $$

So:

Population increased by 200 million people.

Ranking

Largest absolute population increase → rank 1:

$$ rank_{\downarrow}(\Delta P) $$

Example:

Country	Population change
A	+200M
B	+50M
C	+5M
D	−2M

Ranking:

A
B
C
D
Important limitation

This ranking is size-sensitive.

A very large country can add many people even if its population grows relatively slowly.

For example:

Country A: +100M people, +8%
Country B: +20M people, +25%

A has the greater absolute increase.

B has the greater relative growth.

Neither should be presented as "better."

That is exactly why Basis B and Basis C both exist.

Basis C — Period Population Growth (%)

For a stock, the scale-neutral comparison is:

$$ PopulationGrowth_i= \left( \frac{P_{i,E}}{P_{i,S}}-1 \right)\times100 $$
Example

Suppose:

$$ P_{2004}=100M $$ $$ P_{2014}=125M $$

Then:

$$ \left(\frac{125}{100}-1\right)\times100 = 25\% $$

So:

Population increased 25% between 2004 and 2014.

This is the most appropriate basis for scale-neutral cross-country comparison.

Ranking for Basis C

Higher population growth → rank 1.

$$ rank_{\downarrow} \left( \frac{P_E}{P_S}-1 \right) $$

Example:

Country	Population growth
A	+25%
B	+12%
C	+3%
D	−4%

Ranking:

A
B
C
D

This is mathematically comparable across countries because it is a percentage rather than a count.

Annualized population growth

For long periods, I would show an annualized equivalent alongside Basis C:

$$ CAGR_i= \left( \frac{P_E}{P_S} \right)^{1/(E-S)}-1 $$
Example

Population:

$$ 100M\rightarrow125M $$

over 10 years.

Cumulative:

$$ +25\% $$

Annualized:

$$ (1.25)^{1/10}-1 \approx2.25\% $$

So the UI can say:

Population growth: +25.00%
Annualized: +2.25%/year

Is this another basis?

No.

I would keep it as a secondary representation of Basis C, because for a fixed period:

$$ CAGR=f(\text{period growth}) $$

and the transformation is monotonic.

Therefore they produce exactly the same ranking.

Adding both as separate bases would create an artificial extra methodology without adding a different analytical question.

Why I would NOT create a "cumulative population" basis

This is the biggest population mistake to avoid.

Do not calculate:

$$ P_{2005}+P_{2006}+\cdots+P_{2014} $$

and call it:

cumulative population

That would be the sum of annual population snapshots, not the number of people.

Population is a stock.

So:

$$ P_{2024} $$

is meaningful.

But:

$$ \sum P_t $$

is not "population accumulated."

You could technically interpret it as population-years, but that is a completely different demographic exposure measure and has no reason to exist in your general Population family.

Do we need "average population" as a basis?

I would not make it a canonical basis.

You could mathematically calculate:

$$ AveragePopulation= \frac{\sum P_t}{N} $$

but for your site's purpose it does not answer a fundamentally different population question from annual size.

It would also tempt users to interpret it as "population during the period" when it is actually the arithmetic average of annual mid-year estimates.

So I would leave it out.

Observed comparison

For annual population:

Selected year \(t\)

Require:

$$ P_t $$

If valid, country enters the annual universe.

Observed period comparison

For Basis B and C:

Require only:

$$ P_S $$

and

$$ P_E $$

because both are endpoint stock calculations.

For example:

2004 → 2014

requires:

2004 population
2014 population

It does not mathematically require 2005, 2006, ..., 2013.

That is fundamentally different from FDI, remittances, or average inflation where intermediate years are part of the formula.

Like-for-like comparison

Suppose:

Period 1

2004 → 2014

requires:

$$ P_{2004},P_{2014} $$
Period 2

2014 → 2024

requires:

$$ P_{2014},P_{2024} $$

Therefore the common like-for-like universe is:

$$ Valid(P_{2004}) \cap Valid(P_{2014}) \cap Valid(P_{2024}) $$

So both decades are ranked using exactly the same countries.

Example of the like-for-like logic

Suppose:

Country	2004	2014	2024
A	✓	✓	✓
B	✓	✓	✗
C	✓	✓	✓
D	✗	✓	✓
2004→2014 observed

A, B, C, D? No.

Need 2004 and 2014:

A, B, C

2014→2024 observed

Need 2014 and 2024:

A, B? no, C, D

So:

A, C, D

Like-for-like

Intersection:

A, C

Both periods are recalculated using:

A + C

This prevents universe changes from contaminating the comparison.

Country-group filtering

Keep your established sequence:

$$ CountryGroup \rightarrow DataEligibility \rightarrow Calculation \rightarrow Ranking $$

For example, if the selected group is South Asia:

select documented South Asian economies;
apply population-data eligibility;
calculate the population basis;
rank within that universe.

Don't calculate global ranks and then filter them to the region.

Benchmark

Population benchmark should simply be descriptive.

For annual population:

$$ Benchmark_i=mean(P_j) $$

For absolute change:

$$ Benchmark_i=mean(\Delta P_j) $$

For percentage growth:

$$ Benchmark_i=mean(Growth_j) $$

with the focus country excluded.

Then:

Basis A
$$ Gap=P_i-Benchmark $$

unit = people.

Basis B
$$ Gap=\Delta P_i-Benchmark $$

unit = people.

Basis C
$$ Gap=Growth_i-Benchmark $$

unit = percentage points.

Example:

India:

$$ +18\% $$

Peer average:

$$ +10\% $$

Gap:

$$ +8\text{ percentage points} $$

Meaning:

India's population grew 8 percentage points more than the eligible-economy average over the period.

Not:

"India performed 8% better."

Ranking direction
Basis	Ranking	What it actually ranks
A. Annual population	Largest → #1	Population size
B. Absolute population change	Largest increase → #1	Number of people added/lost
C. Population growth %	Highest growth → #1	Relative population growth

For population, there is no economically universal "better/worse" direction.

Higher growth can have very different implications depending on age structure, employment, fertility, migration, resources, etc.

So your UI should not say:

"Higher is better."

It should say:

Higher = larger population / larger increase / faster relative growth.

One subtle issue: migration, fertility and mortality

Population change is the result of demographic components including:

births/fertility,
deaths/mortality,
migration.

The World Bank metadata explicitly notes these components and that population estimates are dependent on them.

Therefore:

$$ P_E-P_S $$

should not be labeled:

"births"

or:

"natural population increase."

It is simply:

net population change

because the change incorporates all demographic components.

Another important issue: estimates vs exact census counts

The World Bank series consists of population estimates, and its metadata notes that such estimates can involve demographic modeling and associated uncertainty.

So the UI should preferably say:

Population

or

Estimated population

rather than implying that every annual figure is an exact headcount.

Final frozen methodology
Population — 3 canonical bases
Basis A — Annual Population
$$ P_t $$

Example:
India 2025 = 1.46B.

Rank: largest population first.

Purpose: size.

Basis B — Period Population Change
$$ P_E-P_S $$

Example:
1.10B → 1.30B:

$$ +200M $$

Rank: largest absolute increase first.

Purpose: how many people were added/lost.

Basis C — Period Population Growth
$$ \left(\frac{P_E}{P_S}-1\right)\times100 $$

Example:
100M → 125M:

$$ +25\% $$

Rank: highest percentage growth first.

Purpose: scale-neutral population growth comparison.

Secondary display:

$$ CAGR= \left(\frac{P_E}{P_S}\right)^{1/(E-S)}-1 $$

Example: +25% over 10 years → ~2.25%/year.

Period convention

Because Population is a stock:

Annual: selected year.

Period change: Start → End.

Period % growth: Start → End.

So:

2004 → 2014 uses 2004 and 2014, not 2005–2014.

That is the correct contrast with your flow families.

In one sentence

Population gets 3 bases: annual size, absolute Start→End change, and relative Start→End growth; CAGR is a display form of the third, not a fourth basis.

That keeps Population simple while still preventing the two major methodological errors: summing population across years as though it were a flow, and confusing absolute demographic change with relative population growth.
