For Exchange Rate, I would be much stricter than for the other families. The raw exchange-rate level is not cross-country rankable, because the numerical size of a currency unit is arbitrary. The useful cross-country comparison is the percentage movement of the exchange rate, because that is dimensionless and invariant to a simple change in currency denomination.

The World Bank indicator is PA.NUS.FCRF, Official exchange rate (LCU per US$, period average). It is an annual average exchange rate, expressed as local-currency units per US dollar, sourced from the IMF International Financial Statistics.

Exchange Rate family
Metric

Official exchange rate (LCU per US$)

Let:

$$ ER_{i,t} $$

= country's annual-average official exchange rate in local currency units per US dollar.

Example:

India: ₹83 / US$
Japan: ¥150 / US$
Euro area: €0.92 / US$

Those three numbers must not be ranked against one another as raw levels.

Why?

Suppose a country redenominates its currency:

100 old units = 1 new unit.

Then:

$$ ER_{old}=100 $$

could simply become:

$$ ER_{new}=1 $$

without the underlying economic value of the currency changing by 99%.

Mathematically, if the unit is rescaled by a constant \(k\):

$$ ER'_t=kER_t $$

the level changes, but:

$$ \frac{ER'_E}{ER'_S}-1 = \frac{kER_E}{kER_S}-1 = \frac{ER_E}{ER_S}-1 $$

So percentage change is invariant to currency-unit denomination.

That is the central rule for this family.

I recommend 3 canonical bases

But only 2 of them are legitimate cross-country ranking bases.

Basis	What it measures	Cross-country rank?
A. Annual official FX level	LCU per US$	❌ No
B. Annual nominal FX change	Year-over-year appreciation/depreciation vs USD	✅ Yes
C. Period nominal FX change	Start→End movement vs USD	✅ Yes

For Basis C, I would also display an annualized rate (CAGR) as a secondary number, but I would not treat it as a separate ranking basis, because for a fixed period it produces exactly the same cross-country ordering.

Basis A — Annual Official FX Rate
Formula
$$ A_{i,t}=ER_{i,t} $$

Unit:

LCU per US$

Example:

Country	2024 official FX
India	₹83 / US$
Japan	¥150 / US$
Euro area	€0.92 / US$

It would be completely misleading to say:

Japan ranks above India because 150 > 83.

Or:

India is weaker than the euro because 83 > 0.92.

The numerical magnitude reflects the denomination of the local currency, not its economic strength.

Therefore

No global ranking.

No cross-country benchmark.

You can display the annual value for the selected country and use it in charts, but not rank countries on it.

The World Bank itself describes this series as a conversion ratio, and notes that exchange rates have limitations for cross-country economic comparisons.

Basis B — Annual Nominal FX Change

This is where cross-country comparison becomes legitimate.

Because the series is quoted:

$$ LCU/US\$ $$

an increase means one US dollar buys more local currency.

Therefore:

Positive %

= local-currency depreciation against USD

Negative %

= local-currency appreciation against USD

Formula:

$$ FXChange_{i,t} = \left( \frac{ER_{i,t}}{ER_{i,t-1}}-1 \right)\times100 $$

Example:

India:

$$ 80\rightarrow82 $$ $$ \frac{82}{80}-1=+2.5\% $$

Interpretation:

2.5% nominal depreciation against the US dollar.

Another country:

$$ 110\rightarrow105 $$ $$ \frac{105}{110}-1=-4.55\% $$

Interpretation:

4.55% nominal appreciation against the US dollar.

This direction is consistent with IMF presentations that express nominal depreciation as the percentage change in local currency per dollar.

Cross-global ranking

Here ranking is mathematically valid, because the percentage change is dimensionless.

Suppose:

Country	Annual FX change
A	−8%
B	−2%
C	+3%
D	+25%

This can legitimately be ordered from:

greatest appreciation → greatest depreciation

or vice versa.

But the UI must never call rank #1 "best currency" or "strongest economy."

The metric only says:

how the country's official nominal exchange rate moved against the US dollar.

Recommended ranking label

Nominal FX change vs USD

rather than:

Currency strength ranking

That distinction is important.

Basis C — Period Nominal FX Change

This should be the main long-period comparison.

Because exchange rate is a price, not a flow, use the Start and End observations directly.

For:

2004 → 2014

use:

$$ ER_{2004} $$

and

$$ ER_{2014} $$

Not 2005–2014.

Formula:

$$ PeriodChange_i= \left( \frac{ER_{i,E}}{ER_{i,S}}-1 \right)\times100 $$
Example 1 — India

Suppose:

$$ ER_{2004}=45 $$ $$ ER_{2014}=61 $$

Then:

$$ \frac{61}{45}-1 = 35.56\% $$

So:

35.56% nominal depreciation against USD over the period.

Example 2 — Country B

Suppose:

$$ ER_{2004}=2.0 $$ $$ ER_{2014}=1.6 $$

Then:

$$ \frac{1.6}{2.0}-1 = -20\% $$

So:

20% nominal appreciation against USD.

Those two percentages are cross-country comparable despite the currencies having completely different denominations.

Why this ranking is mathematically safe

Suppose India changes its currency unit from:

$$ 1\text{ old unit}=10\text{ new units} $$

Both endpoints would scale by the same factor.

For example:

$$ 45\rightarrow61 $$

becomes:

$$ 4.5\rightarrow6.1 $$

The percentage change remains:

$$ \frac{6.1}{4.5}-1=35.56\% $$

So the comparison does not depend on whether the currency has 1, 10, 100, or 1,000 units per dollar.

That is exactly why this basis is appropriate for global comparison.

Secondary display: Annualized FX Change

For a period of \(N=E-S\) years:

$$ CAGR_i= \left( \frac{ER_{i,E}}{ER_{i,S}} \right)^{1/N}-1 $$

Example:

If:

$$ ER_{2004}=80 $$

and

$$ ER_{2014}=100 $$

then cumulative change:

$$ 25\% $$

Annualized:

$$ (100/80)^{1/10}-1 \approx2.26\% $$

So display:

+25.00% cumulative nominal depreciation
+2.26% annualized

This is useful because it gives the user a yearly-equivalent rate.

But don't make it a separate rank

For a fixed period:

$$ CAGR=f(CumulativeChange) $$

and the function is monotonic.

Therefore, if Country A had a larger cumulative depreciation than Country B, it will also have a larger CAGR.

So the rank is identical.

This should be a secondary representation of Basis C, not another independent methodology.

Observed comparison
Annual Basis B

For year \(t\):

Country must have:

$$ ER_{t-1} $$

and

$$ ER_t $$

Then:

$$ \frac{ER_t}{ER_{t-1}}-1 $$

is calculated.

So the observed universe is:

all economies with valid consecutive annual exchange-rate observations.

Period Basis C

For:

S → E

country must have valid:

$$ ER_S $$

and

$$ ER_E $$

No intermediate observations are mathematically required for the endpoint percentage change itself.

This is fundamentally different from FDI.

FDI is a flow:

$$ \sum FDI_t $$

Exchange rate is a price:

$$ ER_E/ER_S $$

So do not use the FDI S+1→E rule here.

Like-for-like comparison

Suppose the user compares:

2004 → 2014

against:

2014 → 2024

Then:

Period 1

needs:

$$ ER_{2004}, ER_{2014} $$
Period 2

needs:

$$ ER_{2014}, ER_{2024} $$

Therefore the like-for-like universe is:

$$ Valid(ER_{2004}) \cap Valid(ER_{2014}) \cap Valid(ER_{2024}) $$

Exactly the same country universe is then used to calculate both period changes.

This is important because otherwise the ranking denominator can change simply because different countries have missing data.

Country-group comparison

Use the same sequence as your other families:

$$ CountryGroup \rightarrow DataEligibility \rightarrow Calculation \rightarrow Ranking $$

Not:

$$ GlobalRank \rightarrow FilterGroup $$

So for a regional comparison:

restrict to the relevant documented country group;
apply the required data-validity rule;
calculate FX movement;
rank within that resulting universe.
Benchmark

There is one special issue here.

For GDP, trade, etc., a mean peer benchmark is usually straightforward.

For exchange-rate changes, the distribution can be extremely skewed because countries experiencing very large nominal depreciations can produce enormous percentage changes.

Imagine:

Country	Period FX change
A	−10%
B	+5%
C	+8%
D	+12%
E	+1,500%

The arithmetic mean becomes:

$$ 301\% $$

That is not representative of any normal country in the group.

Therefore I recommend:

Primary benchmark = leave-one-out median

rather than mean.

For country \(i\):

$$ Benchmark_i= Median(\{FXChange_j:j\ne i\}) $$

Then:

$$ Gap_i=FXChange_i-Benchmark_i $$

This makes the benchmark resistant to a single extreme currency.

I would label it explicitly:

Median eligible-economy FX change

rather than "global average."

That is more statistically defensible for this particular family.

Gap interpretation

Because:

$$ LCU/US\$ $$

has the direction described above:

Positive gap

Country depreciated more than the peer median.

Negative gap

Country depreciated less, or appreciated relative to the peer median.

Example:

Country:

$$ +18\% $$

Peer median:

$$ +6\% $$

Gap:

$$ +12\text{ percentage points} $$

Meaning:

The country's nominal exchange-rate depreciation was 12 percentage points greater than the peer median.

Do not say:

"12% weaker."

Those are different quantities.

What should absolutely NOT be implemented
❌ Ranking raw LCU/USD

For example:

150 yen/USD > 83 rupees/USD > 0.92 euro/USD

This is not an economic strength ranking.

❌ Ranking multi-year average exchange-rate levels

For example:

$$ mean(ER_{2004...2014}) $$

is still denominated in local currency units.

It has the same cross-currency problem.

So I would not even create "Average FX rate" as a cross-country basis.

❌ Calling a depreciation ranking a currency-strength ranking

A country can experience nominal depreciation against USD while having different developments in:

inflation,
productivity,
trade,
real effective exchange rates,
interest rates,
capital flows.

The official FX series alone cannot tell us whether the currency is economically "strong."

The IMF's real effective exchange-rate framework, for example, incorporates trading-partner currencies and relative inflation; that is a different concept from the bilateral nominal USD rate.

❌ Treating percentage change as real currency depreciation

Your metric is nominal, bilateral, and USD-based.

It is not:

real exchange-rate movement,
purchasing-power movement,
export competitiveness,
living-standard movement,
"currency strength" in a comprehensive sense.
❌ Blindly calculating across currency-unit breaks

If a country has a currency redenomination or other unit change within the selected period, the system should verify that the World Bank series is continuous on a comparable unit basis.

If continuity cannot be established:

Data unavailable / period not comparable

is better than generating a spectacular-looking but meaningless percentage.

This is especially important for exchange rates because the raw level is inherently tied to the monetary unit.

One more subtle point: official vs market exchange rate

Your metric is specifically official exchange rate.

The World Bank notes that official exchange rates are determined by national authorities or legally sanctioned markets, and that multiple exchange arrangements can exist. It also notes that market exchange rates are imperfect for comparing real purchasing power and that PPP is a different conversion concept.

Therefore the UI should say:

Nominal official exchange-rate change vs USD

not simply:

Currency performance

That keeps the interpretation tied to what the actual WDI series measures.

Final frozen design I recommend
Exchange Rate — Official FX vs USD

Basis A — Annual Official FX Rate

$$ ER_t $$

Unit: LCU / US$

Cross-country rank: NO

Purpose: annual level for the selected country and charting.

Basis B — Annual Nominal FX Change

$$ \left(\frac{ER_t}{ER_{t-1}}-1\right)\times100 $$

Unit: %

Cross-country rank: YES

Interpretation:

+ = nominal depreciation vs USD
− = nominal appreciation vs USD

Basis C — Period Nominal FX Change

$$ \left(\frac{ER_E}{ER_S}-1\right)\times100 $$

Unit: %

Cross-country rank: YES

Primary long-period comparison.

Also display:

$$ CAGR= \left(\frac{ER_E}{ER_S}\right)^{1/(E-S)}-1 $$

as annualized change, but do not treat it as an independent rank.

So the key architectural rule is:

Raw exchange-rate levels are country-specific units and must not be globally ranked. Percentage changes are unit-invariant and can be globally compared, provided the underlying series is continuous and comparable across the selected period.

That is the safest way to make the Exchange Rate family fit your existing Observed / Like-for-like / cross-globe / benchmark / ranking framework without creating a visually impressive but economically misleading ranking. The World Bank's own metadata supports treating this series as an annual-average LCU-per-dollar conversion rate rather than as a general measure of currency strength.
