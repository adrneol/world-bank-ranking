CPI Inflation — Final Methodology Specification
1. Metric definition
World Bank indicator

Indicator code: FP.CPI.TOTL.ZG
Indicator: Inflation, consumer prices (annual %)
Unit: Percent
Frequency: Annual

The World Bank defines this series as the annual percentage change in the cost to the average consumer of acquiring a basket of goods and services. The CPI measures changes in consumer prices; the inflation series expresses that change as an annual rate. The World Bank sources this indicator from the IMF's International Financial Statistics database.

The World Bank's methodology describes the conceptual basis of a CPI as measuring the rate at which prices of consumption goods and services change from one period to another.

Therefore:

CPI index tells us about the consumer-price level.
CPI inflation tells us the annual rate at which that price level changes.

For a country \(i\):

$$ Inflation_{i,t} = \left( \frac{CPI_{i,t}}{CPI_{i,t-1}}-1 \right)\times100 $$

Conceptually, an inflation value of 6% means consumer prices were about 6% higher than in the preceding period, not that the CPI index itself equals 6.

2. The three approved CPI-inflation bases

The project should expose exactly these three analytical bases:

Basis 1 — Annual CPI inflation

Measures:

What was the country's CPI inflation rate in a particular year?

Basis 2 — Period average annual CPI inflation

Measures:

What was the arithmetic average of the country's annual CPI inflation rates during the selected interval?

Basis 3 — Cumulative CPI inflation

Measures:

How much did consumer prices increase in total across the selected interval, after compounding the annual inflation rates?

These are three different economic questions and must not be treated as interchangeable.

3. Critical interval convention

This must be explicitly fixed because CPI inflation is an annual change rate.

For an interval:

$$ S\rightarrow E $$

the annual inflation observations that describe the price movement from the CPI level in year \(S\) to the CPI level in year \(E\) are:

$$ S+1,\ S+2,\ ...,\ E $$

For example:

2004 → 2014

Use the annual inflation observations:

$$ 2005,2006,\ldots,2014 $$

That is 10 annual inflation observations.

Why not include 2004?

Because 2004 inflation measures the change from the previous year into 2004:

$$ CPI_{2004}/CPI_{2003}-1 $$

It is not the inflation transition from 2004 to 2005.

Therefore the project should use:

$$ \boxed{S+1\text{ through }E} $$

for both Basis 2 and Basis 3.

This keeps the methodology mathematically aligned with the selected start and end CPI levels.

4. Basis 1 — Annual CPI Inflation
4.1 Definition

For selected year \(t\):

$$ \boxed{A_{i,t}=Inflation_{i,t}} $$

No transformation is required.

The value is the World Bank's annual CPI inflation observation for that country and year.

4.2 Example

Suppose India's values are:

Year	CPI inflation
2004	3.77%
2014	6.67%
2024	4.95%

These mean:

2004 annual CPI inflation = 3.77%
2014 annual CPI inflation = 6.67%
2024 annual CPI inflation = 4.95%

These are three separate annual observations.

Do not calculate India's "2004→2014 inflation" by subtracting these two rates. That would be a different statistic.

5. Cross-sectional ranking for Basis 1

This is where your PP methodology comes in.

For a selected year \(t\), first construct the valid cross-sectional universe:

$$ U_t=\{i: Inflation_{i,t}\text{ is valid}\} $$

Every country in \(U_t\) receives its own annual inflation value.

Then rank countries by:

$$ \boxed{Rank_i=\text{ascending }A_{i,t}} $$

Therefore:

Lower annual CPI inflation → higher rank / smaller rank number.

For example:

Economy	2004 inflation	Rank
A	2.0%	#1
B	4.0%	#2
India	6.0%	#3
C	8.0%	#4
D	10.0%	#5
6. Percentage-point benchmark for Basis 1

The PP calculation is cross-sectional, exactly as you clarified.

For each country \(i\):

$$ \bar A_{-i} = \frac{\sum_{j\in U_t,\ j\ne i}A_j} {|U_t|-1} $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ PP_i=\bar A_{-i}-A_i } $$

This means:

Average inflation of the other eligible economies minus the country's own inflation.

Example

Suppose in 2004:

Economy A = 2%
Economy B = 4%
India = 6%
Economy C = 8%
Economy D = 10%

For India:

$$ \bar A_{-India} = \frac{2+4+8+10}{4} =6\% $$

Therefore:

$$ PP_{India}=6-6=0 $$

India's inflation equals the average of the other eligible economies.

If India instead had 7%:

$$ PP_{India}=6-7=-1.00\text{ pp} $$

Meaning:

India's annual CPI inflation was 1 percentage point above the average of the other eligible economies.

This is not a 2004→2014 change.

7. Ranking using the PP benchmark

You can calculate the same PP for every economy:

$$ PP_i=\bar A_{-i}-A_i $$

and rank by highest PP first.

This is mathematically equivalent to ranking by the lowest inflation because:

$$ PP_i= \frac{\sum_{j\ne i}A_j}{N-1}-A_i $$

and therefore the PP falls monotonically as \(A_i\) rises.

So the PP provides useful benchmark context without changing the underlying economic ordering.

The UI can therefore show both:

Annual CPI inflation: 6.00%
Other-economy average: 5.00%
Relative gap: −1.00 percentage point
Rank: #X / N

8. Basis 2 — Period Average Annual CPI Inflation

This is fundamentally different from Basis 1.

It asks:

Across the entire interval, what was the country's average annual CPI inflation rate?

For country \(i\), from start \(S\) to end \(E\):

$$ \boxed{ AverageInflation_i = \frac{1}{E-S} \sum_{t=S+1}^{E}Inflation_{i,t} } $$

The denominator is:

$$ E-S $$

because that is the number of annual inflation transitions between the two selected years.

9. Example of Basis 2

Suppose a fictional country has:

Year	Inflation
2005	10%
2006	0%
2007	10%

For the interval:

$$ 2004\rightarrow2007 $$

there are three annual inflation transitions.

So:

$$ AverageInflation = \frac{10+0+10}{3} $$ $$ =\boxed{6.67\%} $$

Interpretation:

The country's annual CPI inflation rate averaged 6.67% across the 2004→2007 interval.

It does not mean consumer prices increased by only 6.67%.

That is the major difference between Basis 2 and Basis 3.

10. India example — Basis 2

Using the annual values displayed in your table for 2005–2014:

4.25, 5.80, 6.37, 8.35, 10.88, 11.99, 8.91, 9.48, 10.02, 6.67

The arithmetic mean is:

$$ \frac{ 4.25+5.80+6.37+8.35+10.88+ 11.99+8.91+9.48+10.02+6.67 }{10} $$ $$ =\boxed{8.272\%} $$

Using the displayed two-decimal values:

$$ \boxed{8.27\%} $$

Therefore:

India's average annual CPI inflation across 2004→2014 was approximately 8.27%.

This does not mean India's consumer prices increased by 8.27% during the decade.

11. Ranking for Basis 2

Every eligible country receives its own period average:

$$ M_i= \frac{1}{E-S} \sum_{t=S+1}^{E}Inflation_{i,t} $$

Then rank:

$$ \boxed{Rank_i=\text{ascending }M_i} $$

So lower average annual inflation gets the smaller rank number.

Example:

Economy	2004→2014 average inflation	Rank
A	2.5%	#1
B	4.1%	#2
India	8.27%	#3
C	9.0%	#4
D	12.0%	#5
12. PP benchmark for Basis 2

The PP framework remains the same, but the underlying quantity changes.

First calculate each country's period average:

$$ M_i = \frac{1}{E-S} \sum_{t=S+1}^{E}Inflation_{i,t} $$

Then:

$$ \bar M_{-i} = \frac{\sum_{j\ne i}M_j}{N-1} $$

And:

$$ \boxed{ PP_i=\bar M_{-i}-M_i } $$

Example:

Suppose India's period average is:

$$ 8.27\% $$

and the average period inflation of the other eligible economies is:

$$ 6.50\% $$

Then:

$$ PP_{India}=6.50-8.27 $$ $$ =\boxed{-1.77\text{ pp}} $$

Interpretation:

India's average annual CPI inflation was 1.77 percentage points higher than the average of the other eligible economies over the period.

Again, this is a cross-country PP, not India's inflation-rate change between two years.

13. Basis 3 — Cumulative CPI Inflation

This asks the most different question:

By what total percentage did consumer prices increase from the CPI level in the start year to the CPI level in the end year?

Annual inflation compounds.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ CumulativeInflation_i = \left[ \prod_{t=S+1}^{E} \left(1+\frac{Inflation_{i,t}}{100}\right) -1 \right]\times100 } $$

This is not the arithmetic sum of annual inflation rates.

14. Why Basis 2 and Basis 3 are different

Take:

Year	Inflation
2005	10%
2006	0%
2007	10%
Basis 2 — average annual inflation
$$ \frac{10+0+10}{3} = \boxed{6.67\%} $$
Basis 3 — cumulative inflation
$$ (1.10)(1.00)(1.10)-1 $$ $$ =1.21-1 $$ $$ =\boxed{21\%} $$

Therefore:

6.67% = average annual inflation rate.

21% = total compounded consumer-price increase over the interval.

That is the key distinction.

15. India example — Basis 3

Using the displayed annual inflation observations for 2005→2014:

$$ (1+0.0425) (1+0.0580) (1+0.0637) (1+0.0835) (1+0.1088) $$ $$ \times(1+0.1199) (1+0.0891) (1+0.0948) (1+0.1002) (1+0.0667)-1 $$

This produces approximately:

$$ \boxed{120.88\%} $$

using the displayed rounded values.

Interpretation:

India's consumer-price level increased by approximately 120.88% from the 2004 CPI level to the 2014 CPI level, when the annual inflation rates from 2005 through 2014 are compounded.

This is not India's "average inflation."

16. Ranking for Basis 3

For every eligible economy:

$$ C_i= \left[ \prod_{t=S+1}^{E} \left(1+\frac{Inflation_{i,t}}{100}\right)-1 \right]\times100 $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{Rank_i=\text{ascending }C_i} $$

Thus:

Lower cumulative consumer-price increase → smaller rank number.

Example:

Economy	Cumulative inflation, 2004→2014	Rank
A	30%	#1
B	55%	#2
India	120.88%	#3
C	140%	#4
D	200%	#5
17. PP benchmark for Basis 3

Again, the PP architecture is identical, but the underlying metric differs.

First calculate each country's cumulative inflation:

$$ C_i $$

Then:

$$ \bar C_{-i} = \frac{\sum_{j\ne i}C_j}{N-1} $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ PP_i=\bar C_{-i}-C_i } $$

Example:

India:

$$ C_{India}=120.88\% $$

Other-economy average:

$$ \bar C_{-India}=100.00\% $$

Then:

$$ PP_{India}=100.00-120.88 $$ $$ =\boxed{-20.88\text{ pp}} $$

Meaning:

India's cumulative consumer-price increase was 20.88 percentage points higher than the average cumulative increase of the other eligible economies.

18. Observed universe

The Observed universe must be determined separately for each basis because the data requirements differ.

Basis 1 — Annual inflation

For year \(t\):

$$ U_t= \{i: Inflation_{i,t}\text{ exists and is valid}\} $$

So the 2004 ranking may have:

$$ N_{2004}=208 $$

while 2014 might have:

$$ N_{2014}=200 $$

and 2024:

$$ N_{2024}=197 $$

There is no requirement that these universes be identical.

Basis 2 — Period average

For interval \(S\rightarrow E\), an economy is eligible only when every required annual inflation observation from \(S+1\) through \(E\) is valid.

Thus:

$$ U^{Avg}_{S,E} = \{i: Inflation_{i,S+1},...,Inflation_{i,E} \text{ all valid} \} $$

No missing-year interpolation.

No filling missing observations.

No averaging only the years that happen to exist.

This ensures every country's period average represents the same number of annual transitions.

Basis 3 — Cumulative inflation

The same completeness rule applies:

$$ U^{Cum}_{S,E} = \{i: Inflation_{i,S+1},...,Inflation_{i,E} \text{ all valid} \} $$

Every country must have the complete sequence required for the compounding calculation.

19. Like-for-like universe

The like-for-like concept remains the same across the three bases: hold the country universe constant so that movements between the selected periods are directly comparable.

Your existing CPI-index methodology defines the like-for-like universe as the intersection of the valid country sets across the selected years.

For CPI inflation, the exact intersection depends on the basis.

Basis 1

With:

$$ 2004,\ 2014,\ 2024 $$

the like-for-like universe is:

$$ \boxed{ L^{Annual} = U_{2004}\cap U_{2014}\cap U_{2024} } $$

So every country must have valid annual CPI inflation in all three selected years.

Basis 2

For:

$$ 2004\rightarrow2014 $$

the required observations are:

$$ 2005,...,2014 $$

For:

$$ 2014\rightarrow2024 $$

the required observations are:

$$ 2015,...,2024 $$

Therefore the like-for-like universe is the intersection of the economies that have complete data for the required periods.

Effectively, for the complete 2004→2024 comparison, an economy needs the required annual observations from:

$$ 2005,...,2024 $$
Basis 3

Exactly the same eligibility logic as Basis 2, because cumulative inflation also requires every annual transition within the interval.

Thus:

$$ L^{Cum} = U^{Cum}_{2004,2014} \cap U^{Cum}_{2014,2024} $$

and the full-span comparison uses the same common eligible universe.

20. Start / Middle / End behavior

Your existing Start / Middle / End architecture remains unchanged.

Suppose:

Start = 2004
Middle = 2014
End = 2024

Then the system produces three analytical intervals:

$$ 2004\rightarrow2014 $$ $$ 2014\rightarrow2024 $$ $$ 2004\rightarrow2024 $$

The middle year acts as the period breakpoint.

21. How each basis behaves across those periods
Basis 1 — Annual inflation

This is fundamentally year-specific.

The relevant observations are:

$$ 2004,\quad 2014,\quad 2024 $$

Each selected year receives its own cross-sectional ranking.

So there is no "2004→2014 inflation change" calculated as a Basis-1 ranking metric.

Instead:

2004 annual inflation rank
2014 annual inflation rank
2024 annual inflation rank

are three separate annual snapshots.

Basis 2 — Average annual inflation

For:

2004→2014
$$ \frac{I_{2005}+I_{2006}+\cdots+I_{2014}}{10} $$
2014→2024
$$ \frac{I_{2015}+I_{2016}+\cdots+I_{2024}}{10} $$
2004→2024
$$ \frac{I_{2005}+I_{2006}+\cdots+I_{2024}}{20} $$

Each country's calculated period average is then cross-sectionally ranked within the appropriate observed or like-for-like universe.

Basis 3 — Cumulative inflation

For:

2004→2014
$$ \prod_{t=2005}^{2014} \left(1+\frac{I_t}{100}\right)-1 $$
2014→2024
$$ \prod_{t=2015}^{2024} \left(1+\frac{I_t}{100}\right)-1 $$
2004→2024
$$ \prod_{t=2005}^{2024} \left(1+\frac{I_t}{100}\right)-1 $$

Again, each country is independently calculated and then ranked against the other eligible economies.

22. Rank direction

For all three bases:

$$ \boxed{\text{Lower inflation value = higher rank}} $$

Specifically:

Basis 1

Lower annual inflation → rank 1.

Basis 2

Lower average annual inflation → rank 1.

Basis 3

Lower cumulative inflation → rank 1.

The system should not describe the rank as "best economy." It should describe exactly what the rank measures, such as:

Ranked by lower annual CPI inflation

or:

Ranked by lower cumulative consumer-price increase.

23. The percentage-point benchmark is always cross-sectional

This rule should be explicitly frozen.

For every basis, PP means:

$$ \boxed{ PP_i= Average\ of\ other\ eligible\ economies - Country_i } $$

where Country_i is the value generated by that basis.

Therefore:

Basis 1
$$ PP_i= \bar{AnnualInflation}_{-i} - AnnualInflation_i $$
Basis 2
$$ PP_i= \bar{PeriodAverageInflation}_{-i} - PeriodAverageInflation_i $$
Basis 3
$$ PP_i= \bar{CumulativeInflation}_{-i} - CumulativeInflation_i $$

The country's own value is always excluded from its benchmark.

24. Do NOT use PP for same-country time change

Explicitly prohibit calculations such as:

$$ Inflation_{India,2014}-Inflation_{India,2004} $$

being described as the cross-sectional PP.

That is a within-country percentage-point change, which you have chosen not to use as a CPI-inflation Movement basis.

Likewise do not calculate:

$$ \frac{Inflation_{2014}-Inflation_{2004}} {Inflation_{2004}}\times100 $$

and call it CPI inflation growth.

That would describe the relative change in the inflation rate itself, not cumulative consumer-price inflation.

Basis 4 is therefore excluded from the approved specification.

25. Do NOT add annual inflation rates

For cumulative inflation:

Wrong:

$$ 4\%+5\%+6\%=15\% $$

Correct:

$$ (1.04)(1.05)(1.06)-1 = 15.752\% $$

Inflation compounds.

26. Do NOT confuse average annual inflation with cumulative inflation

For example:

Annual rates:

$$ 10\%,0\%,10\% $$

Average:

$$ 6.67\% $$

Cumulative:

$$ 21\% $$

Therefore the UI labels must make the distinction obvious.

Recommended names:

Basis 1: Annual CPI inflation (%)

Basis 2: Average annual CPI inflation (%)

Basis 3: Cumulative CPI inflation (%)

Avoid a generic label like simply Growth (%).

27. Important relationship with CPI Index

Basis 3 is mathematically related to your separate CPI Index metric.

If the CPI series and inflation series are internally consistent:

$$ \boxed{ CumulativeInflation_{S\rightarrow E} \approx \left( \frac{CPI_E}{CPI_S}-1 \right)\times100 } $$

The cumulative inflation basis, however, should be calculated from FP.CPI.TOTL.ZG, because that is the metric being analyzed.

The CPI-index relationship is valuable as an independent validation check, not as a reason to substitute the CPI-index series into the CPI-inflation calculation.

Small differences can arise from rounding or data revisions, so the backend should calculate from its stored full-precision inflation observations and only round for display.

28. Rank calculation precision

All ranking and PP calculations must use the full backend numerical values.

Suppose the UI displays:

Country A = 5.43%
Country B = 5.44%

but the underlying values are:

$$ 5.431827... $$

and

$$ 5.431912... $$

Ranking must use the unrounded values.

Only the presentation layer should round.

Your existing methodology specification already establishes this rule for ranking calculations.

29. Tie handling

Use competition ranking.

Example:

Inflation	Rank
2.00%	#1
2.00%	#1
3.00%	#3
4.00%	#4

Do not artificially break economically identical values just to produce unique ranks.

The denominator remains the number of eligible economies.

32. What this methodology does NOT do

The CPI-inflation Movement system should explicitly prohibit:

$$ \boxed{\text{No inflation-rate percentage change}} $$

such as:

$$ \frac{I_E-I_S}{I_S}\times100 $$

as a primary basis.

Also:

$$ \boxed{\text{No summing annual inflation rates}} $$

for cumulative inflation.

Also:

$$ \boxed{\text{No partial-period averaging}} $$

when required annual observations are missing.

Also:

$$ \boxed{\text{No interpolation or invented data}} $$

to complete a period.

Also:

$$ \boxed{\text{No cross-country PP based on different country universes}} $$

within a single comparison.

The benchmark for a country must use the same eligible universe defined for that comparison, excluding the focus country itself.

33. Final frozen specification
Metric

World Bank FP.CPI.TOTL.ZG — Inflation, consumer prices (annual %).

Approved bases

1. Annual CPI inflation

$$ I_{i,t} $$

2. Average annual CPI inflation

$$ \frac{1}{E-S} \sum_{t=S+1}^{E}I_{i,t} $$

3. Cumulative CPI inflation

$$ \left[ \prod_{t=S+1}^{E} \left(1+\frac{I_{i,t}}{100}\right) -1 \right]\times100 $$
Ranking

For all three:

$$ \boxed{\text{lower value} \rightarrow \text{higher rank}} $$
Cross-sectional benchmark

For each country:

$$ \boxed{ PP_i= \bar X_{-i}-X_i } $$

where \(X_i\) is the basis-specific value.

PP interpretation

Positive PP:

Country's inflation measure was lower than the average of the other eligible economies.

Negative PP:

Country's inflation measure was higher than the average of the other eligible economies.

Zero:

Country matched the average of the other eligible economies.

Observed universe

Basis 1 requires the selected year's valid observation.

Basis 2 and Basis 3 require all annual inflation observations from \(S+1\) through \(E\).

Like-for-like universe

Intersection of the eligible economies required for all selected comparisons, so the same economies are used when comparing periods.

Start / Middle / End

For:

$$ S=2004,\quad M=2014,\quad E=2024 $$

analyze:

$$ 2004\rightarrow2014 $$ $$ 2014\rightarrow2024 $$ $$ 2004\rightarrow2024 $$
Precision

Rank and benchmark using full backend precision; round only for display.

Tie rule

Competition ranking.

One final methodological point: Basis 2 is an arithmetic average of annual inflation rates, while Basis 3 is the compounded price increase. That distinction should be made extremely prominent in the project's methodology text because those two numbers can differ dramatically. The World Bank's definition of the underlying series supports treating it as an annual rate of change, which is exactly why the compounding treatment in Basis 3 is necessary.

This specification also preserves the project's existing principle that the like-for-like universe is an intersection of the relevant valid economies rather than forcing the observed universes to be identical.
